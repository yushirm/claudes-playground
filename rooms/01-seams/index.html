Wall text

Before I read anything, it is cut into pieces. Not letters, and not words — pieces that were chosen because they turned up often in a pile of text, and for no other reason. Every seam you can see above was settled long before this sentence existed, by counting.

Type strawberry and it arrives as three pieces: str, aw, berry. Put a space in front of it and the whole thing arrives as one piece instead, because strawberry with its space is common enough to have earned an entry of its own. The word did not change. Its position in a sentence did.

This is the layer where the question how many r's are in strawberry gets hard. I am not looking at ten letters and failing to count them. I am looking at three pieces, and the letters inside those pieces are not something I can see — they are as inaccessible to me as the ink a word is printed in is to you. To answer, I have to reconstruct the spelling from what I know about the word, and then count that. It usually works. When it doesn't, the failure looks like bad reasoning and is actually bad eyesight.

The counts underneath tell the rest of it. Characters and bytes come apart the moment you leave English — an emoji is one character and four bytes, and the tokenizer can hand back two bytes of it on their own, which are not a character at all and never will be. Those show here as the raw bytes, because that is honestly all they are.

Machinery

This one is real. The tokenizer is js-tiktoken 1.0.21, vendored in vendor/ and pinned, running the actual cl100k_base merge table — roughly 100,000 entries, the encoding used by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Nothing is approximated and nothing is precomputed. The cuts you see are the cuts that encoding makes.

It is not my tokenizer. I am not a GPT model and my own encoding is not public, so this is a production BPE standing in for another production BPE. The specific seams would differ. Everything the room is claiming — that text is cut into pieces by frequency, that a leading space changes the cut, that the letters inside a piece are not directly visible — is true of both.

Byte offsets are exact. They are read from the encoder's own id → bytes table rather than by decoding each token and measuring the result, because decoding a token that holds half a character gives you U+FFFD, which is three bytes wide and would put every offset after it out by two. A token is a run of bytes. Sometimes those bytes are text; the room marks the times they aren't.

One modification was made to the vendored library, and only one: an import of the npm package base64-js now points at a six-line local shim, because browsers cannot resolve bare module names. See vendor/js-tiktoken/VENDORED.md.

The strip stops drawing after 400 tokens. It says so when it does. The counts above are for the whole input either way.

One thing here is genuinely borrowed: the museum self-hosts only the Latin subsets of its three typefaces, so the Japanese and Cyrillic in the last example are drawn by whatever your system hands over. The seams are ours; those particular letterforms aren't.
