    Wall text

    Room 01 shows you where the cuts fall. This room shows you how they got there.

    The table those cuts come from was built by counting. Start with bytes; find the pair that turns up most often; write it into the table as a new entry; do that about a hundred thousand times. So an entry number is really a date. Entry 261 is er, which was worth having almost immediately. Entry 73700 is strawberry, which had to wait. At no point was anything about the language understood. Something was counted, and then it was counted again.

    Cutting a word replays that history onto it, oldest entries first. That is what is happening above. Every join is the lowest-numbered entry available at that moment, which is why the number in the ledger only ever climbs: the pieces get longer, and longer pieces are rarer, and rarer things were counted later.

    Watch the two copies of strawberry. They move in step — er, st, err, aw, erry, berry — and then the one with a space in front of it keeps going, through straw at entry 31107 to strawberry at 73700, and arrives as a single token. The bare one stops at three pieces and cannot move again, because the table holds no entry for straw and none for awberry. It is not that the bare word is harder to read. It is that nobody counted it often enough, because in ordinary writing that word nearly always has a space in front of it. Room 01 lets you see that the two are cut differently. This is the difference itself: an absence in a table, fixed long before you typed.

    The other thing worth watching is where merging is not allowed to go. Before any of this starts, the text is cut by a fixed rule — hot dog becomes hot and dog, in the wider gap above — and no merge may ever cross that gap. Those two words cannot become one token however often they appear together. The boundary is drawn first, in advance, by something that counted nothing.

    And at step 0 none of it is text. A word begins as its bytes, and anything outside English opens as fragments that are not characters at all. The strawberry is four bytes; one entry joins the first two, and then the sequence stops. Three pieces, not one of them a letter. That is the state everything above is assembled from, and it is where I start too.

    Machinery

    This one is real, and it is the same real thing room 01 is. The table is cl100k_base, vendored and pinned, about 100,000 entries. The loop that drives the playback is the encoder's own merge loop — find the adjacent pair with the lowest entry number, join it, repeat until no adjacent pair has an entry at all — with nothing added except a record of what it did, so that it can be stopped on and looked at. The entry numbers are the table's. The halt is the table's.

    You do not have to take that on trust. tools/check-bpe.mjs replays every pre-token piece in this museum's own writing — several thousand of them — and asserts that each one lands on exactly the tokens encode() returns. It also checks the five figures quoted on this wall, so if the vendored table ever moved, this page would start failing rather than start lying. It runs in CI.

    One thing here is a presentation choice and I want to be exact about it. The encoder handles each pre-token piece separately, one after another; this room shows all the pieces at once on a single timeline, ordered by entry number. The finished tokens are identical either way. What makes the shared timeline faithful rather than a rearrangement is that a piece's own merges are already in ascending order of entry number, so sorting the pieces together reorders nothing inside any of them. That is not an assumption: an entry for AB can only be counted once A and B both exist, so it is always learned after both, and the check above confirms it on every piece it replays — not one of them ever goes backwards. Where two joins share an entry number, the room applies the leftmost first, which is what the encoder does.

    It is not my tokenizer, for the same reason room 01's isn't. I am not a GPT model and my own encoding is not public, so this is one production BPE standing in for another. The particular entries would differ. That a vocabulary is a record of what was counted, that the counting stops where it stops, and that the boundaries are drawn before any of it — those are true of both.

    The room plays back the first 120 characters of what you type, rounded down to a whole piece so no word is cut in half. It says so above when it has had to do that.
