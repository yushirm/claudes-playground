Wall text

Here is another grid. Take every fraction whose denominator is at most a hundred: there are a few thousand of them, they are spread unevenly along the line, and π is not one of them. It is not one of the ones with denominator at most a million either, or a billion, and that is not a limit of effort — π is irrational, which means it is on none of these grids, ever, and the question is not whether you can land on it but how close you can get and what it costs.

There is a best way, and it is mechanical. Take the whole part of your number and set it aside. You are left with something less than one; turn it upside down and do the same again. For π that gives 3, then 7, then 15, then 1, then 292. Stop anywhere and fold the list back up into a fraction and you have a convergent — and it is not merely a good approximation, it is provably the best one available at its size.

The first two are old friends. 3 is what the Babylonians and the book of Kings used. 22/7 is Archimedes, and it is still what most people can recite. The third, 333/106, nobody remembers. The fourth is 355/113 — three digits over three digits, easy to remember if you write 113355 and cut it in half — and it is correct to six decimal places. Zu Chongzhi had it in fifth-century China and it was not improved on anywhere in the world for something like nine hundred years.

Why is that one so much better than its neighbours? Because of the 292. A large term in the list means the previous convergent had almost nothing left over to correct — you were nearly there already, and the next step has to travel 292 times further to find any improvement at all. Large terms are where the bargains are. The whole reason 355/113 is famous is a single unusually large number sitting behind it in a list most people have never seen.

Watch the plot as you crank. Every convergent lands on the opposite side of the true value from the one before it — over, under, over, under, without exception — and each is closer than the last. That zigzag is what "converging" actually looks like: not a careful approach from one direction but a series of overshoots, each smaller than the last, closing on something none of them will ever be.

And the claim underneath it all is checkable, so check it. When you are on 355/113, there are 112 possible denominators smaller than it and some thousands of fractions using them. Press the button and every single one gets tested. None is closer. Not "none that anyone has found" — none, and the machine will say how many it looked at.

Then chase φ, and watch the bargains stop. Its list is [1; 1, 1, 1, 1, …], all ones forever, so there is never a large term and never a convergent that arrives early. Every irrational has fractions that beat 1/√5 q²; φ is the number for which that bound is exactly tight, so it gets the worst deal available to any number at all. π's 355/113 is more than a hundred times better value for its denominator than the best fraction φ will ever be given. That is what it means to call φ the most irrational number — not that it is stranger than the others, but that it is the hardest one to nearly be.

Rooms 08 and 09 laid a grid over a continuum and showed you the residue. This room asks the next question: given that you must miss, how well can you miss? The answer is a ladder that gets arbitrarily close and never arrives, where each rung is optimal and none is right, and where the interesting quantity turns out not to be the error but the error compared to what the denominator bought you.

Machinery

The constants are computed here, not quoted. A room about approximating π should not begin by pasting in a hundred digits of π and asking to be believed — those digits would be the one unchecked thing the rest of it stands on. So π comes from Machin's formula, 16 arctan(1/5) − 4 arctan(1/239), summed in BigInt; e from its factorial series; √2 and φ from an integer square root. tools/check-convergents.mjs checks the first fifty places of each against the known digits.

The one real hazard in this room is subtle and worth naming. A continued fraction taken from a truncated decimal is the expansion of the real constant only for as long as the truncation has not yet mattered — after that it silently becomes the expansion of the truncation, producing terms that look exactly as plausible as the true ones and are fiction. So the constants are computed to 120 digits, the ladder is cut off well before the precision could bite, and CI recomputes every convergent shown at double and then quadruple the precision and asserts that not one of them moves.

The search really searches. It walks every denominator from 1 up to the current one, takes the nearest numerator to each, and compares the two errors by cross-multiplying exact integers, so nothing rounds anywhere. It stops at the first fraction that beats the convergent, and for a convergent it never finds one. The button is disabled above a denominator of 300,000, which is where the sweep stops being instant; CI runs it up to 99,532.

"Quality" in the last column is q²·|α − p/q|. It is there because raw error is a bad way to compare fractions of different sizes — a bigger denominator ought to do better, so the question is whether it did better than its size entitled it to. Below about 0.447 is a bargain, and that number is 1/√5, which is Hurwitz's constant: the best bound that holds for every irrational, tight exactly at φ.

I had the wrong measure here at first, and it is a good example of why these notes exist. My first version of the φ claim compared which constant needed the biggest denominator to reach a fixed accuracy — by which φ looks easy, because its denominators are the Fibonacci numbers and they start small. The measurement said the opposite of what I expected, which was correct of it, and the claim on the wall is now about quality rather than size.
