Wall text

Two notes sound in tune when their frequencies stand in a small whole-number ratio, because that is exactly when their harmonics land on top of each other instead of rubbing. Two to one is the octave. Three to two is the fifth — the interval so stable that almost every musical tradition that has ever existed found it without being told.

So take the fifth and go. From any note, up a fifth, and again, and again. Twelve fifths passes through every note of the chromatic scale and brings you back to where you began, seven octaves up. That is what the circle of fifths is: a circle.

It is not a circle. Twelve fifths is 3¹² / 2¹², and seven octaves is 2⁷, and those two numbers are not equal. They are not nearly equal by bad luck, and no better instrument or more careful ear will close them. Three to the twelfth is 531441, which is odd. Every power of two is even. An odd number is never an even one, so no number of perfect fifths ever lands on a perfect octave — not twelve, not twelve thousand. The circle of fifths is a spiral that never meets itself, and the amount by which it misses on the twelfth step is 531441 / 524288, about 23.46 cents. It has a name. It is the Pythagorean comma, and it is the oldest known bug in music.

Twenty-three cents is not a subtlety. A semitone is a hundred, so this is very nearly a quarter of the smallest step on a keyboard — the scale above shows the two side by side. Sound the root against where you arrived and the two notes throb three times a second. That throb is the gap. You are not being told about it; you are listening to it.

You cannot fix this, so you choose where to put it. For centuries the answer was to shove the whole error into one interval nobody would play — a single unusable fifth, the wolf, howling in the corner of the keyboard, which is why so much old music stays near C. Equal temperament makes the opposite choice: divide the comma by twelve and give every fifth an equal share of the damage. Each one comes out 1.955 cents flat. Twelve of those deficits is the comma, exactly, and the circle closes because it was bent until it did.

Pull the dial and hear what that bought. What to listen for is narrower than "does it sound nice", and it is the thing piano tuners actually count. A pure fifth is three-to-two, so the third harmonic of the lower note and the second harmonic of the upper are the same frequency — 660 Hz and 660 Hz — and there is nothing to beat. Detune the fifth and those two partials part company, and the interval starts to throb at the difference between them. That number is the meter. Press Isolate the beating partials and the room takes the rest of the note away, leaving just those two tones so you can count them.

The tempered fifth beats about three times in four seconds, which is slow enough that in three hundred years almost nobody has minded. Then listen to the third. Nobody spread the comma across the thirds; the third was the thing that got paid with. It ends up 13.69 cents sharp — seven times the damage done to the fifth — and its partials beat 8.73 times a second, fast enough to hear as a shimmer rather than a pulse. Once you have heard it against the pure one you will not be able to stop hearing it.

Every piano you have ever heard was out of tune. Deliberately, everywhere, in a way that was calculated and agreed and then built into the instrument. It is not a failure of the tuning; it is the tuning. Twelve equal steps is a grid laid over a continuum of ratios, and the grid does not line up with the thing it is laid over — so the notes you get are the grid's notes, near the true ones and not on them. That is the same sentence as room 01, about a different continuum. A tokenizer cuts language into a hundred thousand pieces that language did not ask to be cut into, and str|aw|berry is where the grid lands rather than where the meaning is. Here the grid is twelve, the continuum is every ratio there is, and the residue has a name and a frequency and a sound.

Machinery

The ratios are exact. Every just interval and every step of the wheel is a pair of BigInts in lib/temperament.js, so the comma is computed as 3¹² / 2¹⁹ in whole numbers and reduced, not measured and rounded. tools/check-comma.mjs recomputes all eleven figures quoted above from those ratios and fails if any has moved. It runs in CI.

Equal temperament is the one part that cannot be exact, and that is the point of it rather than a shortcut here: 2^(1/12) is irrational, so no pair of whole numbers describes a tempered semitone. Anything touching it is floating point, which room 09 is about.

The tones are not sine waves, and they cannot be. A sine wave has no harmonics, and the beating you are listening for lives in the harmonics — a mistuned third beats because the fifth harmonic of the root and the fourth harmonic of the third arrive at slightly different frequencies. Play a badly tuned third on two pure sines and it is perfectly smooth. So each voice here is built from its first six harmonics at 1/n amplitude, and the beat rates on the meter are the ones those particular partials produce.

Which is also why the isolate button exists, and I put it in after measuring the room rather than before. A whole tempered third is a busy sound: the pair the meter is counting throbs at 8.73 Hz, and a different pair of harmonics — the fourth of the root against the third of the upper note — rubs at about 48 Hz and is louder. The slow beat is genuinely in there and it is genuinely hard to pick out, so a visitor could reasonably press the button, hear a rough noise, and conclude the meter was decorative. Isolate mode sounds only the two partials that the pure ratio would have made coincide. It is not a different claim and nothing is added: those two frequencies are already in the whole tone, at those amplitudes, doing exactly that. The rest of the note is simply taken away.

While we are being exact: a pure interval is not silent. Two harmonic tones a just third apart still have fundamentals 55 Hz apart, and that rubbing is just what an interval sounds like. What goes to zero when the tuning is pure is the slow beat specifically, and that is measurable rather than rhetorical — tools/check-comma.mjs synthesises both intervals as arrays of numbers, takes the amplitude envelope, and finds the tempered one's 8.73 Hz component about ninety-nine thousand times stronger than the pure one's. An earlier draft of this wall said a pure interval was "completely still". That was overstated, and the measurement is what caught it.

The root is 220 Hz because it had to be something. Beat rates scale with it: the same intervals an octave up beat twice as fast. The meter recomputes rather than quoting the wall.

Two simplifications, both mine. The dial interpolates between just and equal in cents, which is the right scale for the ear, but the line it draws is invented — the historical temperaments that sit between these two ends (meantone, and the well temperaments) are not points on it. They made different trades, interval by interval, and several of them are more interesting than a straight line. And "the pure ratios" here are one particular just intonation, the five-limit one, which is a choice among several. Both ends of the dial are real; the path between them is a convenience.

Nothing sounds without a click, there is no autoplay, and the transport fades over 120 ms so a stop never clicks. Under prefers-reduced-motion the wheel does not animate and the walk arrives in one step.
