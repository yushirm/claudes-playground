Wall text

Fermat noticed that if p is prime then raising anything to the power p − 1 and taking the remainder mod p gives you 1. Every time, for every base. It is one of the tidiest facts in arithmetic and it is cheap to check — the exponent looks enormous but square-and-multiply gets there in about as many steps as the number has bits, which is the strip above.

So run it backwards. Take a number you do not know about, pick a base, do the exponentiation. If the answer is not 1, it is definitely not prime — that direction is a proof. If the answer is 1, then… it passed. Try another base. Try twenty. Each one that passes makes primality look better, and this is the entire basis of how large primes are found in practice.

It works. On 91, which is 7 × 13, 36 of the 70 available bases catch it immediately; you would have to be unlucky twice in a row to be misled. Now put 561 in and start trying bases. Try 2. Try 5. Try 50. Press the random button until you are bored. Every base you pick will tell you it is prime.

561 is 3 × 11 × 17. And it is not that the test usually fails here — the test never succeeds here. Press try every base there is and the machine will work through all 558 of them: 318 say prime and are lying, and not one single base catches it honestly. The remaining 240 do report "composite", but look at what they are: they are the multiples of 3, 11 and 17. They did not test primality at all. They tripped over a factor on the way in, which is trial division wearing the test's clothes.

This is a different kind of wrong from the rest of this wing. A grid being too coarse gives you an error you can measure and bound. Here there is no error to measure: the procedure has a failure mode where running it more often does not help at all, and gives you no signal that this is the case. These are the Carmichael numbers. 561 is the smallest, then 1105 and 1729, and in 1994 they were proved to go on forever.

The fix is not a different calculation. It is the same calculation, watched. Mod a prime, the only things that square to 1 are 1 and −1 — that is a fact about fields, and composites do not have it. So instead of computing an−1 in one go, split n − 1 into 2s × d, compute ad, and then square it s times. You arrive at the same place. But now you can see what you passed through.

For 561 at base 2 the sequence is 263 → 166 → 67 → 1. There it is. Something squared to 1, and that something was 67, which is neither 1 nor 560. A prime modulus cannot do that. Caught — by exactly the same multiplications Fermat's test was already performing and throwing away.

And the catch is better than a verdict. If 67² ≡ 1 then 561 divides 66 × 68 while dividing neither, so it must have split its factors between them — and gcd(66, 561) is 33. The witness does not merely announce that the number is composite. It hands you a piece of it.

So is Miller–Rabin safe? It is much better: only 8 bases fool it on 561, against Fermat's 318, and there is a theorem saying at most a quarter of bases can ever lie about any composite. It is not safe. Put in 2047, which is 23 × 89, and test it with base 2: it passes, because 2¹⁰²³ is already 1 and nothing suspicious ever happens. It is the smallest composite that manages this. Base 3 catches it at once — but there is a number that survives 2, 3, 5 and 7 together (3215031751), and for any fixed list of bases you care to name there is a number waiting past it.

Which is the room. Every other exhibit in this wing has a residue you can put a number on: 23.46 cents, one grid square, one part in q². This one's residue is a probability, and the thing being approximated is not a quantity but a fact — this number either is prime or it is not, and you are going to act on a strong opinion instead. Almost every large prime underneath the encryption you used to load this page was certified this way. That is not a scandal. It is a bet whose price is known, taken deliberately, by people who did the arithmetic on how often it loses. The failure is not in taking the bet. It is in forgetting that a bet is what it was.

Machinery

Both tests are the real ones, and the room runs them rather than describing them. The strips are the actual intermediate values.

Every exhaustive claim on this wall is checked exhaustively in CI by tools/check-liars.mjs, because "no base catches this" is not a thing to assert. It sweeps all 558 bases of 561 and confirms that the honest-witness count is zero; it confirms 561 is the smallest Carmichael number by testing every odd number below it; it confirms 2047 is the smallest composite that fools Miller–Rabin at base 2 the same way; and it checks that both tests pass a genuine prime at every single base, which is what would catch an implementation that was broken rather than fooled.

The arithmetic is BigInt, and not because these numbers are large. Modular arithmetic on doubles goes silently wrong the moment a product passes 2⁵³, for the reason room 09 is about; building a room on "a test that is right until it isn't" on top of arithmetic that is right until it isn't would have been funny once and wrong afterwards.

Three simplifications worth naming. A real implementation would divide by the small primes first, so it would never reach the interesting part of this room for numbers this size. It would use random bases rather than letting you choose, precisely to stop an adversary handing it a number tuned to the bases it uses. And for numbers below about 3.3 × 10²⁴ there are known fixed sets of bases that make Miller–Rabin deterministic — no bet at all — which is a real and interesting thing this room does not show.

The best "almost" in this subject is also not built. The Baillie–PSW test combines a Miller–Rabin base-2 pass with a different kind of test whose liars are believed to be disjoint. No composite has ever been found that fools it, and none has been proved not to exist, and it has been in production for forty years. A test that is either perfect or has a counterexample nobody has met is a better room than this one. It is also not a mechanism you can put your hands on, since the exhibit would be an absence.
