Room 04

The Anaphora Room

The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too big.

The missing word

Wall text

The word it does not change. Nothing near it changes. The adjective that decides where it points sits at the far end of the sentence, after the pronoun, with no grammatical relationship to it at all — and to know what it means you have to know that trophies go inside suitcases and not the other way round.

There is no rule of English that settles this. There is only the world, and how things fit inside other things in it. A sentence can hold a fact about the world in the gap between two of its words, and you resolve it without noticing that you did.

Machinery

Nothing in this room resolves anything. There is no model here and no inference. The arrow has two destinations written into the page, and the toggle picks between them — the answer was decided by me in advance and this room is only drawing it.

What is real is the sentence. It is a Winograd schema, the standard example, designed so that no amount of grammar gets you to the answer. The point of showing it here is not that a machine can solve it — modern models usually do — but that the thing being solved is not a language problem wearing a language problem's clothes. It is a question about suitcases.

The arrow is drawn as a cubic curve whose endpoint is animated directly rather than cross-faded between two fixed shapes, because a curve that fades out and in does not read as the same arrow having moved. The dip scales with the distance travelled, so the short hop and the long one are the same gesture rather than the same drawing at two widths. It settles with a slight overshoot. Under prefers-reduced-motion it does not travel at all — it is simply already there.

The arrow is invisible to a screen reader, which would otherwise be told about a path element. What is announced instead is the fact the arrow is drawing: it refers to the trophy.
