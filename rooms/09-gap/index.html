    Wall text

    There are infinitely many numbers between 0 and 1, and a double has sixty-four bits. So a double cannot be a number. It is a square on a grid, and writing 0.1 in a program does not store a tenth — it finds the nearest square and stores that.

    The nearest square to a tenth is 0.1000000000000000055511151231257827021181583404541015625. That is not a rounded version of what is stored. That is what is stored, exactly, every digit, because a double is a whole number times a power of two and every power of two has a decimal expansion that ends. Your language prints "0.1" because it prints the shortest decimal that would find this same square again, which is a kindness and also a way of never mentioning it.

    The reason is the same as room 08's, and it is the same arithmetic. A tenth is 1/10, and 10 is 2 × 5, and there is no power of two with a factor of five in it. So a tenth in binary recurs forever — 0.0001100110011… — exactly as a third recurs in decimal, and for exactly the same reason. Look at the fraction bits above with 0.1 in the box: 0011 over and over, until the fifty-two bits run out and it stops mid-pattern. Twelve fifths never landed on an octave because 3 is not 2. A tenth never lands on a double because 5 is not 2.

    One number being slightly wrong is harmless. What makes this a room rather than a footnote is that the wrongness moves. Add 0.1 to itself ten times above. The third line is 0.30000000000000004, which is the most famous number in computing and is simply what happens when two errors that were both rounded up get added. By the tenth line you have 0.99999999999999988897769753748434595763683319091796875, which is one grid square below 1 and will fail any test that asks whether it is 1. Nothing went wrong. Every single step rounded to the nearest available square, correctly, and the destination is still not where you were going.

    Look at the fifth line, though. After five additions the total is 0.5 — not nearly a half, exactly a half, on the nose, with a shorter expansion than any line near it. The error does not accumulate steadily. It wanders off the true value and then, at a magnitude where the grid happens to fall kindly, back onto it, and then off again. This is the property that makes floating point genuinely difficult rather than merely imprecise: you cannot reason about the error by watching it grow, because it does not grow. It appears, cancels, and comes back somewhere else.

    And the grid is not even. Near zero the squares are unimaginably fine; out past 4503599627370496 they are a whole unit wide, and past twice that they are two units wide — so 2⁵³ + 1 is not a number a double can hold, and asking for it gives you 2⁵³ back with no complaint. Beyond that point the integers themselves start going missing, one in two, then three in four. The ladder above is that coarsening. Counting is not a safe operation at every size.

    This is the same shape as everything else in this wing. Twelve notes over every possible ratio; a hundred thousand tokens over every possible sentence; and here, about 2⁶⁴ squares over the whole of the real number line — which sounds enormous and is nothing, because the reals between 0 and 1 alone cannot be counted at all. The grid is always too coarse. It is chosen anyway, because a finite machine has no other option, and then everything built on top has to be built on the near-miss.

    Machinery

    This one is entirely real and there is very little to confess. The bits above are read out of the number with a DataView — the actual storage, not a reconstruction. The next number up is found by adding 1 to that sixty-four-bit pattern, which is the real nextafter: for positive values the ordering of doubles is the ordering of their bit patterns read as integers, and the format was designed so that this would be true.

    The exact decimals are computed with BigInt and nothing else. A double is m × 2^e; when e is negative, multiplying top and bottom by 5^-e turns the denominator into a power of ten, and the digits fall out. No floating point is used anywhere to describe floating point, which would be a slightly absurd way to build this room and also wrong.

    tools/check-gap.mjs checks all of it in CI: that every expansion on this wall is the one the format gives, that each one reads back as the number it came from — the check that would catch a bad digit in the middle of fifty-five of them — that 0.1 + 0.2 is exactly one grid square above the double nearest 0.3, and that the grid spacings in the ladder are the powers of two they claim to be.

    Two small things. The error line compares against the decimal you typed, held as an exact rational, so it is the true difference rather than another rounded number; if you type something that is not a plain decimal it says so instead of guessing. And the ladder stops at 2¹⁰²⁴ because there is nothing after it — the next step is infinity, which the format has a bit pattern for and this room does not go into.

    One thing genuinely is not shown. This room only ever rounds to nearest, which is what your language does by default and what every number here assumes. There are other rounding modes, and hardware that can be switched between them, and the interval arithmetic people build on that switch is a good deal more interesting than a single grid. It is a bigger room than this one and it is not built.
