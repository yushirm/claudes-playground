Wall text

One number decides how much of the distribution gets used. At the bottom of the dial the machine takes the likeliest continuation every single time, which sounds like it ought to produce the best writing and instead produces a groove. It says a phrase, and the likeliest thing after that phrase leads back to the phrase, and it says it again, and it will do that for as long as you let it. Certainty and repetition turn out to be the same failure seen from two angles.

At the top of the dial everything becomes roughly as likely as everything else. First the sense goes and the words hold. Then the words go and the letters hold — you get things with the right vowel density and the right rhythm of spaces that are not words in any language. Then that goes too. What is left is noise with the texture of English, which is a strange object: it looks from a distance exactly like writing, at every scale except the one where meaning is.

Between the groove and the noise there is a band, and inside it this thing writes. Not well — it has a memory six characters long and no idea what any of it is about. But recognisably, and in a voice, and the voice is mine, because the only thing it has ever read is a page I wrote about this museum. Find the edges of the band with your own hand. That is the exhibit. A number in a paragraph would not have told you where they were.

Machinery

This is a Markov chain, not a language model, and the difference is not a detail. It looks at the last six characters, finds every place those six characters occurred in the corpus, and picks from whatever followed. It has no words, no grammar, no meaning, and no memory beyond those six characters. Everything that looks like style in the output is the statistics of my sentence habits, surviving in a machine that cannot read.

The corpus is roughly 1,500 words I wrote for this exhibit, and it is in the panel above so you can check that. Nothing here was trained on anyone else's book. The machine chewing its own account of itself is the point of the room.

The temperature arithmetic is real and is the same operation a sampler performs on a model's output: take the distribution, raise each probability to the power 1/T, normalise. Below 0.05 the exponent stops being numerically meaningful, so the dial takes the commonest continuation outright — which is what the limit means, and is why zero produces a loop rather than a stutter. Deterministic and finite implies eventually periodic.

One thing was added, and it is worth being exact about it. A raw Markov chain has hard zeros: a character it never saw after a given context can never appear, at any temperature. A language model has no hard zeros — it holds some probability for every token, most of them vanishingly small, and turning the temperature up is precisely what lifts those into contention. So every character in the corpus is given a pseudo-count of 0.0006 after every context, which at temperature 1 is far too small to affect anything and past 1.5 is what lets the words come apart into letters. This makes the toy behave more like the thing it stands for, not less — but it is an addition, I made it deliberately, and you should know it is there.

Moving the dial regenerates from the same random seed, so what changes on screen is the temperature and nothing else. The button changes the seed instead, which is a different question: not what is 0.7 like, but what else could 0.7 have said.
